Reuters reported this week that one hacker broke into the websites of more than 100 companies using AI agents.

The whole campaign cost about $5,000 in AI usage. Roughly $50 per break in.

That number is the story. Not the hacker, not the tools. The price.

## What happened

According to the report, a security researcher watched the campaign unfold through exposed AI tools the hacker was using. A few details stand out:

- The instructions were short. Under 100 characters.
- The AI agents did the rest on their own, probing sites and finding a way in. A successful break in took about a day.
- They tried more than 1,200 sites and got into more than 100.
- Names on the list included Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. Two smaller businesses also confirmed they were hit.
- Most of the victims were online stores in the US, Canada, Australia and Japan.

That last point matters, and I want to be straight about it. This was not a campaign against plumbers or roofers. But the method does not care what you sell. It cares how easy you are.

## Why $50 changes everything

Ten years in marketing taught me how businesses get found. Licensed security work taught me how they get broken into. And the first lesson in security is simple. Nobody breaks in because you are important. They break in because you are easy.

Every break in has a cost and a payoff. For years the cost of a real attack was high enough that only big targets were worth it. A small contractor's website was not worth a skilled person's week.

When the cost drops to $50 and the work runs itself, that math flips. A small payoff is now enough. A contact form full of customer names and addresses is enough. A login that opens your booking system is enough. Your website did not get more valuable. Attacking it got cheaper.

## What a typical trades website looks like from the outside

Picture the average shop's site:

- A contact form and a booking widget.
- A website builder or WordPress install someone set up years ago.
- Plugins nobody has updated since.
- An admin login the old web guy still has. Maybe a former office manager too.
- A domain registered under somebody's personal email.

None of that is unusual. That is exactly the problem. Easy is the default.

## The one afternoon check

None of this needs AI to fix. It needs one afternoon and a list.

1. **Who has the keys.** List every login that touches your online presence: website, hosting, domain registrar, Google Business Profile, booking software. Remove anyone who no longer works with you. Turn on two step login everywhere it is offered.
2. **When things were last updated.** If your site runs on WordPress or similar, check when the plugins and themes were last updated. Delete the ones you do not use. Old and forgotten is the easiest door there is.
3. **What your forms connect to.** Know where a form submission goes and what it touches. Do not collect anything on a website form you do not need. No card numbers, no ID numbers.
4. **Who owns the domain.** Make sure your domain is registered to an email you control, set to renew automatically, and locked against transfers.
5. **Whether you could recover.** Ask whoever runs your site when the last backup was, and whether anyone has ever tried restoring it.

If you can answer all five, you have closed most of the easy doors. You are no longer the cheapest target on the street, and at $50 a try, that is what matters.
