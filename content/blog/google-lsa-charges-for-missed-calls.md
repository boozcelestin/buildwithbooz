Google started charging you for calls you never picked up.

As of October 1, 2026, if you run Local Services Ads, a missed call during business hours counts as a paid lead. The caller only has to stay on the line for more than 20 seconds. You do not have to answer. You do not have to call back. You still pay.

That is a quiet change with a loud effect. Here is what changed, why it matters more than it looks, and what to check this week.

## What actually changed

Before October 1, a missed call with no voicemail only became a billable lead if you called the person back. If the call rang out and you never returned it, it cost you nothing on paper.

Now the rule is simpler and less forgiving:

- A call during your business hours that you do not answer is chargeable if the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds.
- Follow up calls that meet Google's valid lead rules can be charged too.
- If your line uses a "press 1 for service" style menu, the 20 second clock starts after the caller presses a key. If they never press one, there is no charge.
- Google says it is adding protection against spam and robot calls, but it has not said much about how that works yet.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Ten years running Endgame Marketing, I sat in a lot of meetings about cost per lead. Owners would argue for an hour about whether a lead should cost $40 or $60.

Then a lead would call at 4:50 on a Friday. It would ring out. Nobody mentioned it again.

That call was never free. A homeowner with a dead AC or a leaking water heater does not wait. They call the next shop on the list, and the job goes with them. The cost was real every time. It just never showed up on an invoice.

Now it shows up on the invoice.

So Google did not create a new problem. It put a price tag on an old one. A missed call used to cost you the job. Now it costs you the job and the lead fee.

## The fix is not a cheaper ad

The instinct will be to cut the budget or chase a lower cost per lead. That misses it. The money is not leaking in the ad. It is leaking in the minutes after the phone rings.

Three questions decide whether this change hurts you:

1. **Who answers between open and close?** Not in theory. On a Tuesday when two techs are out and the owner is on a roof.
2. **What happens at lunch?** If the office steps out at 12:30, every call in that window is now a paid lead going to voicemail.
3. **Who calls back, and how fast?** A missed call you return in two minutes is often still a job. One you return at the end of the day usually is not.

If nobody clearly owns those three things, you are paying Google for your own voicemail.

## What to check this week

- **Pull your missed call count since October 1.** Compare it to the same weeks before the change.
- **Check your business hours in LSA.** The billing rule follows the hours you set. If your listed hours are wider than the hours someone actually answers, you are paying for the gap.
- **Look at your call routing.** Know what a caller hears in the first 20 seconds, and whether a person ever picks up.
- **Download your lead history.** Google is moving Local Services Ads inside the main Google Ads platform over the next year, and your old performance reports will not come with it. Save them now while you still can.

## The bigger point

This is the clearest example yet of something I keep coming back to. Most service businesses do not have a lead problem. They have a problem with what happens after the lead shows up.

Google just made that problem show up on the bill.
